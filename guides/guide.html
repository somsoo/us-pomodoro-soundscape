Using the Pomodoro timer and background noise

1. The Pomodoro Technique in brief

The method was developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s and is named after a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. The basic steps are:

  1. Decide which single task you will work on.
  2. Set a timer for 25 minutes and work only on that task until it rings.
  3. Take a 5-minute break, preferably away from the screen.
  4. After every four pomodoros, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

2. Choosing your lengths

The timer starts with 25 minutes of focus, a 5-minute short break and a 15-minute long break. You can change each one. Longer blocks can also work for some tasks; Duke University's Academic Resource Center gives 52 minutes of work and 17 minutes of break as one example. Try a length for a few days and adjust it.

3. White, pink and brown noise

White noise has the same power at every frequency and sounds like bright hiss. Pink noise has the same power in every octave, so its power falls about 3 dB per octave and it sounds softer. Brown noise falls about 6 dB per octave and sounds like a deep rumble. The page makes all three in your browser and sets them to the same average level. Many people use steady noise to cover background sounds; pick the one you find least distracting and keep the volume low.

4. How the focus log counts

Only focus sessions that run to the end are logged. Each one adds its full length to the local date on which it ends, and your streak counts the days in a row with at least one finished focus session. The log stays in your browser and is never sent to us.

Source for the Pomodoro steps and the 52/17 example: Duke University Academic Resource Center, "Pomodoro Technique" (September 24, 2025), checked September 30, 2026.
